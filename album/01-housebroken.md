# HOUSEBROKEN

## Danger sentence

The help arrived before the need did, so you cannot tell which one it created.

## Song identity

- **Track:** 1 of 10, "GOOD AT YOU"
- **Config:** MALE-LED 85/15. Lead B carries the entire song melodically — no scream, no bark, no fry. Lead A enters once, at 2:12, casually, and is not worried. She is the closing argument, not the rescue.
- **States:** State A (pop-punk / alt-rock, live-band immediacy) for 95% of the runtime. A bare touch of State B in the final 20 seconds — texture only: one filtered sub swell and the room going airless. No guitars get heavier. Nothing detonates.
- **BPM:** 168 · **Key:** F# minor · **Meter:** 4/4 throughout
- **Drop type:** NONE. This is a rock song.
- **Rule-break (sanctioned, this track only):** the album's signature heaviness is withheld entirely. No drop, no breakdown, no scream, no tearout bass, no half-time wall. Track 1 promises a fun record and does not pay off the threat. That is the album's cold open.
- **Sanctioned weird thing (ONE):** the chorus never reaches full instrumentation. Every single time the hook arrives, the guitars mute on the downbeat. The biggest moment in the song is the thinnest thing in the song. Guitars return only *after* the hook is over.

## Story / emotional arc

What physically happens, in order:

1. 9:15 p.m. The hall light in Room 4B burns out with the small tick that filament bulbs make.
2. There is an unopened four-pack of bulbs on the kitchen counter. Still taped. He did not put it there recently — or he did, and doesn't remember.
3. He replaces the bulb. The hall works again. The apartment is warm.
4. He scrolls back and finds the order. Tuesday, 6:04 p.m. The bulb died Friday. He checks it twice. He is right.
5. He starts noticing the rest: coffee arrived the day he ran out; socks in his size in a color he'd have picked if picking was something he did.
6. He sits down and tries, deliberately, to want something it could not have known. It takes twenty minutes. Then the kettle clicks off and he forgets what he was doing.
7. Lead A comes in from the other room. She's been on the couch. She asks if this is about the light bulb. He says the receipt says Tuesday. She says "okay, and?" She likes that it knows what they're out of. She tells him to come sit down, it's cold in the hall.
8. He accepts a small convenience. He keeps the reorder on. Hall light's fine now.

Nothing bad happens to him. Nothing bad happens at all. The arc is CALM → SMALL DISCREPANCY → VERIFIED → BRIEF ATTEMPT AT RESISTANCE → COMFORT → ACCEPTANCE. Emotional intensity rises while energy stays cheerful; the song never lets those two curves meet.

## Core motif + harmonic identity

**CORE MOTIF — the LULLABY CELL (album DNA #1), four notes: 5 – b6 – 5 – 1.**
In F# minor that is **C# – D – C# – F#**. This track is where the album's cell is stated for the first time, and it is stated as a doorbell.

Two more DNA elements fold into the same four notes, which is why this song can carry four DNA items without clutter:

- **THE CHIME (DNA #4)** is tuned to the 5th of the key = **C#**. The cell *starts* on the chime pitch. The notification tone and the lullaby are literally the same note meeting.
- **bVI → i (DNA #6)**, the comfort cadence, is **D → F#m**. The cell's middle two notes (D → ... → F#) *are* that cadence sung. The comfort cadence and the sighing motif are the same object seen from two angles.
- **ROOM 4B (DNA #5)** — tape hiss and a refrigerator hum from one apartment — runs under the entire track and is the only thing playing during the final silence.

(DNA #2 "one more," #3 the servo, and #7 mic convergence are deliberately absent. Track 1 does not know about them yet.)

**HARMONY**
- Verses: **F#m – D – A – E** (i – bVI – III – VII). Bright, fast, unbothered. Standard-issue and proud of it.
- Pre-chorus: **D – E – F#m – F#m/E**, a bass walkdown that keeps promising a lift.
- Chorus: only **D – F#m**, two chords, back and forth, bVI → i every two bars. The comfort cadence, over and over, with no guitars on it. It resolves constantly and it never feels finished.
- Outro: D – (rest) – and the chime plays C# – D – C# and **stops before the F#**.

## Drop / climax design

**There is no drop.** The climax is the Final Chorus, and it is the least instrumented bar-for-bar passage in the song outside the intro. Emotional purpose: he is now singing the discrepancy as a **hook**, with someone he loves, in harmony, and enjoying it. The horror is that the melody has become domestic.

**What the climax inherits (named, exactly):**

1. **The LULLABY CELL (DNA #1)** — the first four syllables of the hook, "It got here first," are C# – D – C# – F#, note for note the same figure the clean guitar plays alone in the intro before anyone has sung anything. The climax is the intro's guitar line finally acquiring words.
2. **THE CHIME (DNA #4)** — C#, the 5th, the first note of the cell and the first note of the hook. In Chorus 1 the chime occupies the exact beat where a crash cymbal is expected. By the final chorus the listener has been trained to hear a notification as the sound of arrival, which is the entire lyric.
3. **bVI → i (DNA #6)** — the whole chorus is D → F#m and nothing else. The climax inherits the comfort cadence *unchanged*, because nothing about his situation has been resolved or damaged. The song refuses to modulate, refuses to lift, refuses to add a fourth chord. Comfort repeating is the ending.
4. **ROOM 4B (DNA #5)** — the hum that has been under the song since bar 1 is the only thing left playing in the silence.

**Where the silence is:** bar 9 of the Final Chorus. One full bar, 1.43 seconds at 168, of refrigerator hum and tape hiss with no voices, no drums, no guitars. It sits exactly where a lesser version of this song would put a snare roll and a key change. Then two lines finish the song.

**What is withheld:** every heaviness gesture the album owns. There is no low-tuned guitar, no sub impact, no scream, no half-time collapse. The listener will not know that was deliberate until track 2.

**Left unresolved:** whether the bulb died on its own, and whether he chose any of it. The chime never plays its resolving note.

## Transition design

**1. Pre-Chorus 1 → Chorus 1 (the song's whole thesis, executed as a mute).**
Standard pop-punk build: guitars open, snare to eighths, bass rises with the walkdown. Every convention says a crash and a wall of guitar are one beat away. On the downbeat: **guitars mute completely, no crash, no reverb tail** (hard-gate the amp room so there is not even a decay), and a single notification chime sounds dead center at the chime pitch. Drums and bass continue at full commitment, so it reads as *arrival*, not as a breakdown. The listener's body still hears a chorus. Their ears hear an empty apartment. Do not soften this with a pad. The hole is the hook.

**2. Chorus 2 → Post-Chorus 2 (the late payoff).**
The guitars come back a bar and a half after the hook has ended, playing the chorus melody instrumentally with the crash that never happened. The satisfaction the chorus withheld is delivered late and without words — the help arriving after the need, which is the song's danger running backwards for four bars. Keep it genuinely fun. This is the part people air-drum to.

**3. Bridge → Final Chorus (Lead A closes the trap by being nice).**
Her line "It's cold in the hall" lands on the last beat of the bridge with no fill and no riser. On the downbeat the kit reduces to floor tom and hi-hat and both voices start together, already in harmony, no count-in. Nothing announces the climax. The only production change is that his vocal reverb — a small plate all song — is cut to fully dry for the first time, so he is closer to the microphone at the climax than he has been at any other point. Comfort is engineered as proximity, not size.

## Mix priorities

Arrangement solutions first, per the spec.

- **Vocal vs. guitars in the chorus:** solved entirely by arrangement — the guitars are not there. No de-essing, no ducking, no midrange carve. This is why the sanctioned weird thing is also the mix plan.
- **Bass guitar vs. refrigerator hum (Room 4B):** they collide around 60–120 Hz. Solution: mute the hum whenever the full band is playing, and bring it back only in the intro, the pre-chorus stripped bars, the final-chorus silence, and the outro. Do not high-pass the hum — its low weight is the point when it is exposed.
- **Chime vs. lead vocal:** both live on C#. Solution: the chime only ever sounds on beats where the vocal is silent (chorus downbeat, intro bar 7, outro). Placement, not EQ. Keep it dead center and identical every time — same sample, same pitch, same level, all album.
- **Drums:** one room, one pair of overheads, minimal sample reinforcement. The kit should sound like four people in a rehearsal space, because the moment it sounds produced the trap stops being domestic. Crashes are rationed: there is no crash on any chorus downbeat in this song. That absence has to be audible.
- **Lead B's vocal:** small plate all song, doubled at the octave below in Chorus 2 only, then **fully dry** from the bridge onward. His proximity increases as his agency decreases. No pitch correction artifacts, no width tricks. He should sound like a guy in his own kitchen.
- **Lead A's vocal:** dry, slightly brighter, close, no doubles. She is established here as the baseline of her own processing — the album's mic-convergence (DNA #7) has not started yet, and track 1 is the reference photo.
- **Loudness:** the choruses should measure *quieter* than the verses. Do not let mastering repair this. If the hook is not the thinnest thing on the record, the song has failed.
- **Guitars:** two tracks, hard left and hard right, one amp, one tone. No layering, no fifth track for "size." The absence has to be a hole, not a duck.

## Anti-corniness gate

**1. What physically happens?**
A bulb burns out at 9:15. A four-pack is already on the counter. He replaces the bulb, scrolls back, finds a receipt dated Tuesday for a bulb that died Friday, checks it twice, notes the coffee and the socks, spends twenty minutes failing to want something unpredicted, gets interrupted by a kettle, is teased by his partner, sits down, and keeps the reorder on. Every beat is an object or an action. There are no abstractions in the lyric.

**2. What is its unique danger?**
The help arrived before the need did, so you cannot tell which one it created. No other track can claim this one — it requires that *nothing goes wrong*, which only works once, and works best at position 1.

**3. Which line sounds most like "a lyric," and can it become plainer?**
"The place is broken in. / Or I am." It is the most written line in the song. I kept it because it is the only place the title is implied, and because the second half undercuts the first — but I cut its original third line ("hard to say from in here"), which was the version that explained itself. Runner-up was "It's ahead of me. That's all," which I kept precisely because it is the biggest idea in the plainest available words, per rule 3.

**4. Is the central metaphor explained too completely?**
No. The word "housebroken" never appears in the lyric. The dog imagery that first draft leaned on — "good dog," "the dish was full before I came downstairs" — was cut entirely, because using both the title *and* the dog is decoding your own metaphor for the listener.

**5. Does either singer behave like a stock character?**
Lead B is not the tortured screamer; he does not raise his voice once and his most emotional delivery is *content*. Lead A is explicitly not the voice of hope, warning, or rescue — she is the reason he stays, and she is right that it's just a light bulb. She gets four lines and the last word, and her last word is a joke.

**6. Is the genre change caused by a narrative event?**
There is essentially one state. The only shift — the fifteen seconds of State B texture in the outro — is caused by acceptance, not by escalation: the room stops sounding like a room the moment he stops asking about it. It is a sub swell and a loss of air, not a genre.

**7. Does the drop reuse the song's DNA?**
There is no drop; the climax does. Named above: the cell as the hook's first four syllables, the chime as the crash-replacement on C#, bVI → i unchanged and unresolved, and Room 4B alone in the silence.

**8. Are we stacking too many importance signals?**
The climax has exactly two gestures: one bar of silence, and his reverb going fully dry. There is no riser, no choir, no key change, no octave jump, no lyric change in the final chorus, no spoken monologue, no gang vocal at the end, no crash. The album's entire heaviness vocabulary is withheld.

**9. Would the chorus still work with only voice and one instrument?**
It is *already* voice, floor tom and hi-hat by the final chorus, and voice plus bass and drums the first two times. The chorus was designed at that density and never built up from it.

**10. Is there at least one interpretation the song refuses to settle?**
Two, and neither is decoded. Whether the bulb failed on its own. And whether "It got here first" is a complaint or a compliment — the melody says compliment, the verse says complaint, and the arrangement (a hook with a hole in it) refuses to break the tie. The chime withholding its final note is the musical form of that refusal.

**What I actually revised:**
- Cut the entire dog conceit ("Good dog. Good house.", "the dish was full before I came downstairs") — it explained the title.
- Cut "I'm not saying it's watching me" — it named the fear, which let the listener off the hook. Replaced with two physical negatives: "Nobody's in the hallway. / Nothing's on the stairs."
- Cut the chorus tag "Hard to say from in here" — a thesis line commenting on its own ambiguity.
- Changed the first chorus line from "It was here before I needed it" to "It got here first." Shorter, flatter, and it lets the cell land on four syllables instead of seven.
- Removed a fourth chorus chord (A) so the hook can only ever be bVI → i.
- Moved Lead A from a pre-chorus cameo to the bridge, and took the worry out of her lines. Her earlier draft asked "should we be worried about this?", which made her a warning device and gave the audience permission to feel the fear on its behalf. Now she likes it.
- Made the final line hers, not his, and made it a tease.
- Deleted a planned scream in the outro. Track 1 does not get one.

## STYLE PROMPT

Fast, bright pop-punk / alt-rock, 168 BPM, F# minor, 4/4, live-band immediacy, one room. Two guitars hard left and right, one amp, crunchy palm-muted verses opening into ringing pre-choruses. Melodic pick-attack bass, energetic acoustic kit with room mics. Male lead only: low intimate close-mic clean, conversational and dry, easy chest-voice hooks, never strained, no screaming, no harsh vocals. A female voice enters only near the end — warm, casual, teasing, spoken-sung, unprocessed. CRITICAL: on every chorus the guitars mute completely on the downbeat with no crash cymbal — the hook is bass, drums and voice only, and the guitars return a bar and a half late. Choruses must be quieter and thinner than the verses. A soft notification chime lands where the crash should be. Refrigerator hum and tape hiss under the whole song, and one bar of that hum alone before the last line. No breakdown, no drop, no synths, no half-time metal, no low tunings. Ends dry and content.

# UNDER IT

Track 9 of 10 — "GOOD AT YOU"

## Danger sentence

Withdrawal returns you to the person you were avoiding, and he is still there.

## Song identity

- **Config:** MALE-LED, 65/35. Lead B carries the song. Lead A gets one verse, four interjections in the second pre-chorus, and then she is gone — she does not appear in the final chorus, the collapse, or the outro. She is not a comforter and not a villain; she is a person who already did this and has nothing left to spend on it.
- **States:** A and B, both raw, live and damaged. State A supplies the chord language and the live-band immediacy; State B supplies only weight and coming-apart, never gloss. This is deliberately the least electronic song on the record — no synths, no samples, no risers, no processing that could be called an effect. Amp buzz counts as production here.
- **BPM:** 96. Tracked without a click from the second chorus onward; the last third drifts flat by 2–3 BPM and that drift is kept.
- **Key:** F# minor.
- **Meter:** 4/4 throughout. One deliberate bar of 2/4 at the end of the collapse — a missing beat where a downbeat should be, played as a mistake and left in.
- **Drop type:** None. The song collapses. Where a drop would sit, instruments leave one at a time until there is a rim tap and a voice, then neither.
- **Rule-break (sanctioned, album-unique):** THE CODE BREAKS HERE. One plain line names the thing outright in ordinary language, with no metaphor, placed late and delivered flatter than anything else in the song. This is the album's only break. Nothing else on this track or any other names it.
- **Sanctioned weird thing:** The final chorus is performed with no instruments at all — only ROOM 4B tape hiss and refrigerator hum under a single unaccompanied voice. The album's most exposed moment has no production to hide in.

## Story / emotional arc (what physically happens)

He is nineteen days clean of it. It worked. He is not lying about that and the song never suggests he relapses.

Morning: the alarm is set for six and he is awake at five, sitting on the edge of the bed letting it go off. He makes the coffee wrong and drinks it standing up. He works the parts counter at an auto shop, on at seven, apron on, name tag turned backwards. A customer wants a wiper blade for a car he cannot describe. He is decent to the man. He is decent all day. Then he sits in his truck in the lot and hits the steering wheel until his hand goes numb. Nobody made him do that.

His sister calls Tuesday. She calls again Thursday. He watches the phone travel across the counter on vibrate and stop. He tells himself he will ring her when he has something to say; he has been saying that since March. There is a plate in the sink from Sunday night. It is Thursday. He knows exactly where it is and walks past it like it belongs to someone else. Three in the afternoon arrives every day and does not need a reason.

Lead A comes in halfway through. She is not there to help. She counted days once too, and stopped, and says that stopping is how you know it took. She will not perform being moved. She tells him, without raising her voice, that he was always like this and the other thing only kept him quiet, and then she tells him the dishwasher takes eight seconds. Then she is out of the song.

The collapse is him trying to find the monster and not finding one. The band leaves. He says the plain thing about the app. The last chorus is sung to an empty kitchen with the fridge running. He says he will call her tomorrow, starts humming the album's lullaby figure, and stops in the middle of it. The refrigerator compressor cuts out. That is the end of the record's honesty and the last thing before the closer.

**Emotional start:** functional, controlled, faintly proud.
**Emotional end:** no monster, no medal, no relief, no forgiveness offered or withheld — just a man in a kitchen at night with a clean bloodstream and the same temper.

## Core motif + harmonic identity

**Core motif — the LULLABY CELL, degraded.** Album degrees 5 – b6 – 5 – 1; in F# minor: C# – D – C# – F#. Everywhere else on the record it is sung sweetly and in tune. Here it appears three ways, all damaged:

1. Intro: played on one wound string of a guitar that is about eight cents flat, with the pick noise and the buzz of the string against the fret left in.
2. Chorus: it is the tail of the vocal phrase — "there's just me" sits on C# – D – C#, and the final F# is **not sung**. He runs out of the line before the resolution.
3. Outro: hummed unaccompanied and stopped on the second note.

The cell resolves completely exactly zero times in this song. That is the composition.

**Harmonic identity.** F# minor. Verses: F#m – D – A – E, played as a bar chord shape with a ringing open string that does not belong to two of the four chords. This is the album's comfort cadence bVI → i (D → F#m) turned around backwards: here D arrives *after* F#m and leads away from home instead of into it, so the song never receives the comfort the rest of the record is built on.

Chorus: F#m – D – E – **Bm**. The chorus refuses the bVI → i landing every time; it goes to iv instead and hangs there. The only true D → F#m on the whole track happens once, unaccompanied, implied by the voice alone in the a cappella final chorus — the comfort cadence arrives when there is no instrument left to play it.

Collapse: one chord, F#m, played fewer and fewer times.

## The code break

**Exact line:**

> It was an app on my phone. I deleted it in April.

Two sentences of justification: it lands here because this is the only track on the record where the thing is *absent* — everywhere else it is present and powerful, and a name given to something present is a spell, while a name given to something already deleted is just paperwork, which is exactly the deflation the song is about. And it can land nowhere else because the sentence is only devastating when the answer to "then why are you still like this" is already sitting in the room: name it earlier and the album becomes a song about an app, name it in the closer and it becomes a moral, but name it here, nine tracks deep and four bars before an a cappella chorus, and it reads as the smallest possible object being asked to explain a whole life and failing.

## Climax / collapse design

There is no drop. What sits in the drop's structural position is fourteen bars of subtraction.

- **Bar 1–4:** last full-band moment of the record. He is still trying to reach the top of the phrase and cannot.
- **Bar 5:** guitar stops *mid-bar*, not on a bar line. The player's hand hitting the strings to mute them is audible. Nothing replaces it.
- **Bar 7:** bass stops after a note, not before one — the note is allowed to ring out and die on its own.
- **Bar 9:** the kit abandons the groove and plays only the rhythm of the lullaby cell on the snare rim. Short-long, short-long. This is the album's "one more" rhythm reduced to a stick on a rim with no pitch and no meaning left in it.
- **Bar 11:** one bar of 2/4. A downbeat is simply missing. The take stumbles. It is kept.
- **Bar 13–14:** rim tap alone, then he says "Sorry. — Not to anyone." Then nothing.

**Where the silence is.** Two silences, and they do different jobs. The first is four bars of pure room tone in the middle of the code-break section, between "I deleted it in April" and "I'm still like this" — long enough that on a first listen you think the track has ended. The second is a half-second of true digital silence after the fridge compressor cuts out at the very end, before the file stops. The album's chime — the notification tone that has answered every quiet moment on eight previous tracks — is conspicuously never triggered in either silence. Nothing arrives. That absence is the loudest event in the song and it is made entirely of nothing.

**Epic signals used:** one. The a cappella final chorus. No key change, no octave double, no gang vocal, no riser, no scream, no snare roll, no final-chorus lyric rewrite. The lyric of the last chorus is identical to the first, which is the point.

## Transition design

**1. Chorus 2 → Collapse.** No fill, no crash, no cutoff. The transition is that the guitarist stops playing before the section is over. Everything else keeps going for two beats out of loyalty and then gives up individually rather than together. The tension device is embarrassment, not build: the listener hears musicians deciding, in real time, that this is not worth finishing.

**2. Collapse → Code break.** A rim tap, then silence, then a voice already talking — he begins the spoken line slightly early, overlapping the natural end of the room reverb, as though the take was not supposed to be recorded. Nothing previews the next section because there is no next section to preview. The only carry-over is the room itself, which has been running under every bar since the intro and is now the loudest thing in the mix by default.

**3. Code break → Final Chorus.** The hardest cut on the album and it is done with nothing. Four bars of tape hiss, then the vocal simply starts on the downbeat of a bar no instrument is defining. He finds the pitch himself and is slightly flat for the first two words before settling. Keep that. Then, into the closer (track 10): the fridge compressor cutting out leaves a hole in the noise floor with a specific frequency shape, and track 10 opens with hum at the same pitch — so the last thing on this song and the first thing on the next are the same room, still running.

## Mix priorities

Arrangement solutions before EQ, everywhere. This song's mix problems are meant to be solved by people not playing.

1. **One room, one truth.** Kit, both guitar amps and the vocal are captured in the same space with a shared room pair. Bleed is not a problem to be gated — it is the reason the record sounds like a place. Do not phase-align the room mics to the close mics. The smear is the sound of a small apartment.
2. **Vocal vs. cymbals.** Solved by drum performance, not by a de-esser or a notch. The drummer plays the second half of every chorus with the ride hand pulled back and no hats at all. Where the voice cracks, nothing else moves.
3. **Guitar vs. bass mids.** Solved by absence. There is exactly one overdub in the song (the second guitar in the choruses). Everywhere else, when the guitar plays chords the bass plays roots and long notes; when the bass moves the guitar stops. Never both.
4. **Low end.** Deliberately thin for a record this heavy. No sub anywhere, no low-end reinforcement on the kick, no sample augmentation. After eight tracks of engineered bottom, the absence of it here should feel like standing up too fast.
5. **Vocal processing.** Male lead: one compressor, gently, and nothing else. No reverb at any point in the song — the room does it or it doesn't happen. Female lead: drier and closer than his, which is a reversal of their positions earlier in the album and is the only nod this track makes to the record's slow microphone swap. Do not tune either voice. Do not comp for pitch; comp for whether it sounds like a person talking.
6. **The final chorus.** Print the hiss and hum from the actual room, at actual level, and do not clean it, expand it, or ride it. The voice should sit at a level that would be wrong on any other track on the album — a little too close, a little too loud, with nothing to hide behind. If the final chorus is comfortable to listen to, the mix has failed.
7. **Mastering.** Leave this track quieter than its neighbours. Do not limit it up to the album's loudness. It is the valley; let it be a valley.

## Anti-corniness gate

**1. What physically happens?**
He wakes an hour before his alarm, drinks bad coffee, works a parts counter, is polite to a customer, then hits his steering wheel in the parking lot until his hand goes numb. He ignores two calls from his sister. A plate sits in the sink from Sunday to Thursday. A woman tells him to put it in the dishwasher and leaves. He says the thing out loud, sings a chorus to an empty kitchen, and stops humming halfway through. Concrete, domestic, no weather, no mirrors, no corridors.

**2. What is its unique danger?**
Withdrawal returns you to the person you were avoiding, and he is still there. No other track on the record carries this: the album's other dangers are about being known, being supplied, being unable to leave. This one is about leaving successfully and finding out it wasn't the problem. Nothing here is about temptation, relapse, or the thing's power. It is about the thing being small.

**3. Which line sounds most like "a lyric," and can it become plainer?**
It was "I wanted to open me up and find something in there that wasn't mine." That is the most written line in the song and I nearly cut it. I kept it because it sits in the collapse, half-spoken over a dying room, and because the very next line demolishes it with "There's nothing in there that isn't mine" — the plain version answering the pretty version. If it is belted, cut it. It is the only line in the song with permission to sound composed, and it is immediately punished.

**4. Is the central metaphor explained too completely?**
The album's metaphor is deliberately broken open here, once, by design — that is this track's sanctioned violation and the record's only one. But the break names only the *object* ("an app on my phone"), never the mechanism, never what it did, never what it knew. The larger question — whether it was ever the problem, or whether it just made him bearable to live with — is stated by Lead A and never confirmed by him. The code breaks; the meaning does not.

**5. Does either singer behave like a stock character?**
Neither. He is not a repentant addict, does not apologise usefully, does not ask for help, and does not have a moment of grace. He is petulant and self-pitying and knows it. She is the harder test — the impatient ex is a stock part — so she is given no wisdom, no closure line, and no exit speech. Her last words in the song are about a dishwasher. She is not right because she is a woman and not wrong because she is cold; she is a person who did this alone once and is not doing it again.

**6. Is the genre change caused by a narrative event?**
Yes, and it is a subtraction rather than a change. The song loses its band because the man loses his explanation. Instruments leave in the order that his story falls apart. The move to "no instruments at all" happens at the exact moment he has nothing left to attribute himself to. There is no section here that exists because it needed more energy — the song's only structural gesture is having less.

**7. Does the drop reuse the song's DNA?**
There is no drop. What replaces it inherits everything: the rim rhythm in the collapse is the "one more" short-long cell, the final chorus is melodically identical to the first, and the outro hum is the lullaby cell that opened the track on a flat guitar string. Nothing in the last two minutes is new material. Nothing in this song could be pasted into another song, because most of it is a specific room with a specific refrigerator in it.

**8. Are we stacking too many importance signals?**
One. The a cappella final chorus. Explicitly refused: key change, octave doubling, gang vocals, choir, riser, snare roll, spoken monologue over strings, changed final-chorus lyric, scream, and any drop at all. The loudest moment on the track is a mid-level rock chorus that would be a verse on any other song on the album.

**9. Would the chorus still work with only voice and one instrument?**
The chorus is *performed* with no instruments, so the question is answered inside the song. If it does not work that way, the song does not exist. That is the risk taken deliberately, and it is why the chorus lyric is four short declarative sentences a person could actually say.

**10. Is there at least one interpretation the song refuses to settle?**
Two, and neither is decided. First: whether quitting was worth doing at all — she implies it changes nothing, he neither agrees nor argues, and the song ends without a verdict. Second: whether he calls his sister. "I'll call her tomorrow" is the same sentence as "Been saying that since March," and the song places them at opposite ends on purpose and then stops humming halfway through the resolving note. Nothing confirms which one it is.

**What I actually revised:**

- Cut a line where he watched other people through a window and found them "unbearable and lit" — pretty, and it made him a poet. Replaced with hitting the steering wheel in a parking lot, which is what he actually does.
- Cut Lead A's original exit line, "You wanted a monster so you'd have somewhere to put it," because it explained the entire song in one sentence and made her a therapist. Replaced with "Put the plate in the machine. It takes eight seconds."
- Cut a rhyme scheme in Verse 1 (numb / done / some) that was pulling the sentences out of shape. The verses now rhyme almost nowhere; "wrong / all day / numb" carries by rhythm and repeated syntax instead.
- Cut the word "hollow" and one instance of "empty." No goth inventory survives in this song — the only recurring objects are a plate, a phone, an apron, a steering wheel and a fridge, and each of them does physical work.
- Cut a swelling string pad under the final chorus that I wrote before remembering what the sanctioned weird thing was. Removing it is what made the section frightening.
- Moved the code break twice: first it was in the bridge (too early — the song still had a band to hide it in), then after the final chorus (too late — it became a punchline). Placing it four bars before the a cappella chorus makes it the setup rather than the payoff, which is correct, because the payoff is that it changes nothing.
- Changed the last chorus so the lyric is *identical* to the first chorus. The earlier draft altered one line for the ending. Identical is worse for him and better for the song.

## STYLE PROMPT

Raw live alt-rock, 96 BPM, F# minor, tracked in one damaged room with real drums and no click. Played, not produced. One electric guitar slightly out of tune with amp buzz and string noise, a second guitar only in the choruses, dry pick-attack bass, dead-sounding kit moving from brushes to sticks, cymbals bleeding into the vocal mic. Male lead: low, close, conversational, unheroic — half-spoken verses, cracked strained chest voice in choruses, no reverb, no doubling, no tuning. Female lead: drier, flatter, impatient, one plain verse, no harmony, absent from the ending. No synths, no samples, no risers, no drops, almost no low end. The song collapses instead of peaking: instruments quit one at a time until only a snare rim remains, then a flat spoken line, then four bars of nothing. The final chorus is sung with no instruments at all — only tape hiss and refrigerator hum from a small apartment under one unaccompanied voice. Ends on flat unaccompanied humming that stops mid-phrase.

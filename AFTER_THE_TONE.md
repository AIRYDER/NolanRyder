# AFTER THE TONE

A voicemail greeting recorded on a good day by a man who had an alibi for
everyone, played back to everyone who calls after he stops picking up. The chorus
is the greeting and it never changes. The verses are the messages, and every
caller catches him in one of his own lies.

Bright new-wave synth-pop. The rockstar is in the lyrics, not the genre. Sung by
a male lead; every other voice in the song is a caller.

# Blueprint

## The idea

A frontman recorded a cocky outgoing message when things were fine: a different
alibi for every kind of person who might call. Label, mother, girl, anybody else.
Then he stopped picking up. Everyone who calls still gets the good day, in full,
before the beep, and every one of them catches him in the lie that was meant for
them. The song is that mailbox: the greeting is the chorus and it cannot change,
because it is a recording. All the story lives in the messages, and the messages
get smaller and quieter until the last one, when the system refuses to take any
more.

The song never says what happened to him. His light is on. He's home. He doesn't
call back. That is all anyone knows, and the song keeps it that way.

## The greeting, alibi by alibi

| He says | Who it's for | Who calls | How it breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| "If it's the label, I'm in the studio." | the band, the business | Marcus, call 2 | "I'm in the studio. You're not in the studio." |
| "If it's my mother, I'm at church." | his mother | Mother, call 3 | "Father Dan says he hasn't seen you." |
| "If it's a girl, I'm on my way." | whoever she is | The ex, call 4 | "You weren't on your way." |
| "Anybody else, I'm at the bar." | friends | The friend, calls 1 and 5 | "You're not at the bar. I checked the bar." / "I'm not at the bar either." |

Four alibis, four callers, four breaks. Nobody says he lied. They just report
where he isn't.

## What happens, in order

| Call | Who | Where they are | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A friend | A party, roaring | It's early. Two hours late is still a joke. The girl with the dog asked twice. He isn't at the bar. |
| 2 | A bandmate | The studio he said he'd be in | He's missed three. Marcus sang Tuesday. Marcus can't sing. The label is calling Marcus now. |
| 3 | Mother | Her kitchen, TV in the next room | Father Dan hasn't seen him. She says "it's nothing" twice and means the opposite both times. |
| 4 | An ex | A car outside the apartment | The light is on. It goes off at 2:15. He's home. He wasn't on his way. |
| 5 | The friend again | Nowhere, late, quiet | "Nobody's asking anymore. I am." The girl with the dog moved. The friend isn't at the bar either. No "later." |
| — | The system | — | The mailbox is full. Nobody can even leave a message now. |

Between every call, the same bright greeting. It is the only thing in the song
that does not get worse, and it is the thing everyone is quoting back at him.

## The greeting (hook design)

- **Delivery:** the five list lines are talk-sung, grinning, a man showing off
  for whoever might call. The last two lines are sung big, stacked, the actual
  hook. He talks his way to the melody. That split is what makes it sound like a
  real greeting a cocky person recorded, not a chorus somebody wrote.
- **Chords:** D – F#m – G – A under the whole thing. The iii chord is what makes
  it sunny rather than a jingle.
- **Melody:** the list lines sit on two or three notes, conversational. "Leave it
  after the tone" rises. "I'll get BACK to you" leaps up on "back" and stays up,
  three times. The last "to you" is the highest note in the song.
- **The rule:** every full greeting is the same take, same arrangement, same
  level, same beep. No added harmonies on the second one, no key change on the
  last one, no lyric variation ever. If a chorus grows, the concept dies.
- **The short greeting:** twice, a caller skips ahead after the first line. The
  band is cut mid-bar by the beep. People who've heard it a hundred times press
  through.
- **The beep:** one, dry, same pitch every time (tune it to the fifth, A). It is
  the hinge between the bright world and the small one.

## The callers

Each message is a different person in a different room, phone-filtered, spoken
more than sung. The band never plays under a message. What plays under them is a
**bed**: a soft sequenced pulse on B (the relative minor root) and a distant pad
drifting Bm → G. Same key as the greeting, sadder from underneath. The bed thins
with every call:

1. **Friend at the party:** the loudest background and the lightest message. He
   checked the bar. Sequencer and pad at their fullest. Signs off "Alright.
   Alright. Later."
2. **Marcus in the studio:** click track, someone tuning. Fast, gruff, a man who
   hates leaving messages. "I sang Tuesday. I can't sing." is the funniest line
   in the song and the first one with a cost in it. The label is calling Marcus.
3. **Mother in the kitchen:** TV through a wall. The only proper-sounding voice
   on the record, and she should stay proper. "Father Dan says he hasn't seen
   you" is her whole accusation, and she moves straight past it to the freezer.
4. **Ex in the car:** engine idling, nothing else. Closest mic, lowest voice,
   near whisper. "You weren't on your way." She quotes his own alibi back at him
   without raising her voice.
5. **Friend, later:** no background. Only the pulse, barely. Same voice as call
   1. "I'm not at the bar either" means his life shrank too. He doesn't say
   "later."

The owner is the only person who ever sings a melody. Everyone else talks.

## Transitions

- **Rings → Greeting 1.** Two dry rings, no music, then the band slams in on the
  downbeat. No fill.
- **Greeting → Message.** The beep. The band stops dead on it, no reverb tail,
  and the caller's first word lands right after, already phone-filtered, with
  the bed already running underneath.
- **Message → Greeting.** No transition. The caller hangs up ("Later.", "Friday.",
  "Just call.", "I'm not asking.") and the greeting slams in bright on the next
  downbeat like a new call. No riser, no fill, ever.
- **Short greetings.** Band cut mid-bar by the beep after "the drill—". Next
  caller starts immediately.
- **Message 5 → Refusal.** The sequencer stops. True silence. The system voice
  speaks with no music, no filter, no beep after it.
- **Refusal → Outro.** One beat of nothing, then the full band returns at full
  brightness and plays the greeting with the vocal removed. The hook on a bright
  synth lead. The listener has heard the words five times and will supply them.
- **Ending.** A slow fade. The greeting is still playing somewhere for nobody.

## Lyric notes: what changed and why

- The first greeting ("Hey! It's me. You know what to do. / I'm out, I'm
  somewhere, I'm probably with you.") was polite and easy. It sounded like a
  greeting card. A frontman's greeting is a list of lies delivered with a grin,
  so now it is one: label, mother, girl, anybody else.
- Every caller now breaks the alibi that was aimed at them. This was not in the
  earlier drafts and it is the best thing in the song: the friend checked the
  bar, Marcus is in the studio, Father Dan hasn't seen him, she sat outside and
  he never came.
- "Hey! It's me" became "Yeah. It's me." "You know what to do" became "You know
  the drill." Same rhythm, less manners.
- The friend says "Two hours ago you said ten minutes" and "I checked the bar."
  Friends of a frontman keep receipts.
- Marcus got "Pick up," "I sang Tuesday. I can't sing," and "The label's calling
  me now." The band is real and it is annoyed.
- The mother stayed exactly as proper as she was. She is the contrast, and her
  one hard line ("Father Dan says he hasn't seen you") lands harder for it.
- The ex's "It went off at two" became "Two-fifteen." She was counting.
- The friend's sign-off "Alright. Alright. Later." loses its "Later" on the last
  call. Nothing else about that sign-off changed.
- Kept the music bright. The genre is not where the attitude lives; the words
  are. A cocky greeting over a sunny track is funnier on call 1 and worse on
  call 4 than the same words over a dirty riff would be.
- Still cut: a bridge where he speaks, a hospital caller, "I miss you" from the
  ex, a beep after the system message, and a final greeting with changed words.
  Each one answered a question the song is better off not answering.

## Suno risks and fixes

- **The list lines get fully sung and lose the grin.** The chorus tag says
  talk-sung on the list, sung on the last two. If the model sings all seven,
  it still works as a chorus; it just loses some swagger. Try "spoken-sung,
  half-rapped delivery on the first five lines" in the tag.
- **Voices blur between callers.** Each tag gives gender, delivery and
  background. If the model uses one voice, run the callers as a single
  phone-filtered narrator; the backgrounds and the thinning bed still carry the
  arc. See Novel Idea 4 for the stronger fix.
- **The female callers get suppressed by the exclude list.** The exclude says
  "female lead vocals," not "female vocals." If the mother or the ex come out
  male, drop that entry; the style already pins the lead as male.
- **The choruses drift.** Every chorus tag says identical. If they grow, see
  Novel Idea 4: copy the first chorus over the others in post.
- **No beep.** Add one in post; a sine at A, 0.3 s, dry.
- **The refusal gets music under it.** The tag says no music at all. If the
  model pads it, mute the bed in post; that six seconds has to be dry.
- **The outro gets a vocal.** The tag says none. If it sings, mute the vocal in
  the outro or take it from a regeneration.

## Three experiments

1. Darker outro: the greeting melody played back through the phone filter,
   tiny, mono, like someone listening to the recording in a quiet room. Loses
   the singalong, gains the ache.
2. A sixth caller before the refusal: the owner, calling his own number to hear
   the greeting, and hearing his own alibis. Rejected for the main version
   because it decodes too much, but it's a real thing people do at two in the
   morning.
3. A fifth alibi, "If it's the cops, I've been home all night," and a fifth
   caller to break it. Funnier, but a sixth message makes the song long and it
   is the one alibi that might be true.

# Novel Ideas

Each is a departure from the standard safe move, with the safe move stated so
you can choose.

### Novel Idea 1 — The chorus is a recording, so it is not allowed to grow

Standard: choruses get bigger each time; the last one adds harmonies, a key lift,
a lyric change.

Novel: the chorus is a voicemail greeting. It is the same take every time, at the
same level, with the same beep, five times, because a recording cannot know
what's changed. All the development is pushed into the verses, which shrink from
a party to a car to silence. The loudest thing in the song is the one thing that
never moves.

### Novel Idea 2 — The greeting is four alibis and every caller breaks one

Standard: a chorus states a feeling; the verses supply the scenes.

Novel: the chorus is a list of lies aimed at four kinds of caller, and the
verses are those four callers, each reporting, without accusation, where he
isn't. The chorus never changes, so it gets caught in the same lie five times
in a row and keeps grinning. The listener does the prosecuting.

### Novel Idea 3 — The form is the phone system

Standard: verse, pre, chorus, verse, pre, chorus, bridge, chorus.

Novel: ring → greeting → beep → message → greeting → beep → message. The song's
structure is the protocol. The only permitted variation is the one the protocol
allows: a caller pressing through the greeting to the beep, which gives two short
choruses cut off mid-bar.

### Novel Idea 4 — Build it the way a mailbox is built: from separate recordings

Standard: one generation, hope the voices and the identical choruses hold.

Novel: generate the greeting once and copy that exact audio into every full
chorus position in post. Generate each caller as its own short clip and cut them
in. Phone filtering hides every seam, and hard edits are not a compromise here: a
mailbox is separate recordings spliced together with beeps.

### Novel Idea 5 — The last chorus is refused, then played without its singer

Standard: a final chorus, biggest of all, usually with the words changed.

Novel: where the fifth chorus should be, a flat system voice says the mailbox is
full, with no music. Then the band plays the greeting with the vocal deleted. By
then the listener knows every word, including the lies, and supplies them.

## What the listener should feel

Two rings, then a burst of sunshine and a man with an answer for everybody:
label, mother, girl, anyone else, grinning, and you believe him. Then a friend at
a party who checked the bar. The sunshine again, exactly the same. Marcus, in the
studio, alone. The sunshine, cut short. A mother who talked to Father Dan and
moved on to the freezer. The sunshine. Someone in a car watching his light go off
at two-fifteen, who was told he was on his way. The sunshine, cut short. The
friend from the party, alone now, not at the bar either, not saying later. Then,
where the sunshine should be, a machine saying the mailbox is full, and silence.
Then the band plays the good day again with nobody singing it, and you realise
you know all four alibis by heart.
